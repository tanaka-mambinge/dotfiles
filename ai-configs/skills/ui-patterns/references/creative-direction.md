# Creative Direction Reference

Read this after the initial design pass for every new or revised site. Use it
as an idea space for challenging generic defaults, not as a template or a
required style menu. Do not enumerate these ideas to the user every time and
do not ask the user to choose from them by default. Select, combine, or reject
ideas based on the product, audience, content, and task.

Use the agent's internal todo or step tool to record the chosen direction and
the creative areas inspected. Do not create a tracking document in the
project.

## Explore the design space

Before finalizing the design, consider alternatives across several of these
dimensions. The goal is deliberate choice and useful variety, not novelty for
its own sake.

### Geometry and silhouette

Consider whether the interface wants strict geometry, softened corners,
circles, arcs, organic contours, irregular shapes, cutouts, capsules,
overlaps, continuous outlines, offset layers, or a controlled mixture. Let
shape support the product's character and content. Do not round everything by
default, and do not square everything by default.

### Layout and composition

Explore how the page could establish hierarchy through asymmetry, full-bleed
content, editorial pacing, modular systems, open negative space, layered
overlaps, framed scenes, strong alignment, flowing sections, or another
composition that suits the task. Do not repeatedly default to the same split
hero followed by the same card grid.

### Surfaces and material cues

A surface may be quiet and flat, but it may also use transparency, blur,
soft-depth lighting, tactile relief, paper-like texture, hard graphic edges,
layered planes, image-as-surface, or other material cues. Use effects to make
relationships and hierarchy clearer, not to decorate every element.

### Buttons and controls

Controls can communicate character through silhouette, weight, outline,
depth, icon treatment, text relationship, grouping, placement, or response.
Consider inline actions, framed actions, circular controls, segmented controls,
floating controls, tactile controls, expressive text actions, and other forms
when appropriate. Keep controls recognizable and accessible, and avoid using
one button recipe everywhere in a page or across every variant.

### Typography and copy expression

Vary the relationship between display and interface type, scale, alignment,
line breaks, density, and copy rhythm when the direction calls for it. Copy
can be direct, conversational, editorial, instructional, playful, restrained,
or another product-appropriate voice. Do not change wording arbitrarily; make
it serve the audience and the chosen direction.

### Imagery and illustration

Images may act as a hero, backdrop, crop, inset, object, texture, sequence,
reference point, or supporting detail. Illustration, diagrams, icons, shapes,
and typographic composition may carry the visual focus when that better serves
the content. Avoid making the same asset the same dominant hero in every
direction.

### Interaction and motion

Consider feedback that moves, reveals, reshapes, follows, layers, masks,
connects, or hands attention between elements. Vary the origin, trajectory,
staging, pacing, and mechanism according to the visual direction and the
frequency of the action. Do not rely on color or opacity alone when the
interaction needs stronger spatial feedback. Use `ui-animation` for detailed
motion craft.

## Apply without bias

- Choose a coherent subset of ideas; do not combine every effect or treatment.
- Do not make a design resemble the inspiration source literally.
- Do not copy a gallery example or reuse one favorite treatment everywhere.
- For variations, avoid repeating the same creative family and inspect the
  rendered directions side by side.
- If the design still looks like a generic collection of rectangles and
  standard controls, revise the shape, surface, composition, or interaction
  decisions before delivery.

The supplied Gumroad screenshot and CSS Scan button gallery are useful sources
of breadth when inspiration is needed. They are references for the range of
possible design decisions, not instructions to reproduce a particular site,
button, or visual style.
